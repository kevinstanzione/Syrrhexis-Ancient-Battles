c. 480–146 BC · From Himera to the fall of Carthage

Carthage began as a colony of Tyre on the coast of what is now Tunisia, and grew into the richest city of the western Mediterranean. For two centuries it fought the Greeks of Sicily, from the disaster at Himera in 480 BC to Timoleon's victory at the Crimisus, where a storm drove the Sacred Band, the best-born citizens of Carthage, into the mud. Then came Rome. Carthage lost the First Punic War and Sicily with it, and its unpaid mercenaries rose against it in a revolt that nearly destroyed the city. Hamilcar Barca rebuilt its power in Spain, and his son Hannibal led an army from there over the Alps into Italy in 218 BC. At the Trebia, Lake Trasimene and Cannae he destroyed one Roman army after another. Italy held, though, and Scipio beat him at Zama in 202 BC. Rome razed Carthage in 146 BC.

Carthage was a city of merchants, and it fought mostly with other people's sons. Its own citizens served as heavy spearmen in emergencies and as officers and generals, and the Sacred Band stood at their head in the wars of the 4th century. The core of its armies were Libyan subjects, long-service spearmen whom Hannibal later armed with Roman gear stripped from the dead at the Trebia and Trasimene. Around them came the hired and allied contingents: Numidian horsemen, Iberian swordsmen and light horse, Gallic warriors and cavalry, Balearic slingers, Ligurian hillmen, Campanian and Greek mercenaries. In front marched the African forest elephants, smaller than the Indian kind, whose drivers carried a chisel and mallet to kill any beast that turned on its own side.
