c. 345–185 BC · From the Nanda kings to the fall of the Mauryas

When Alexander's army reached the Punjab, the soldiers heard of a kingdom on the Ganges with an army of thousands of elephants, and they refused to go any farther. That kingdom belonged to the Nanda kings of Magadha. Around 322 BC a young adventurer named Chandragupta Maurya, guided by tradition by the Brahmin minister Kautilya, overthrew the last Nanda and took over his realm and his army. He went on to drive out Alexander's garrisons, and when Seleucus marched east to win the lost provinces back, Chandragupta forced a treaty on him and sent him 500 war elephants in exchange. His grandson Ashoka conquered Kalinga, and the slaughter there so shocked him that he turned to Buddhism and had his remorse carved into stone across India. The empire lasted until 185 BC, when the last Mauryan king was killed by his own general while reviewing the army.

The Greek ambassador Megasthenes reported a standing army of 600,000 foot, 30,000 horse and 9,000 elephants, paid and equipped by the state. The Arthaśāstra, the great manual of statecraft credited to Kautilya, describes the army's four limbs: infantry, cavalry, chariots and elephants. It ranks troops by their loyalty, from the hereditary Maula down to guild companies and forest tribes, and it recommends spies, poison and deceit as freely as battle. Greek writers described Indian archers with bows as tall as a man, braced against the ground to shoot, and big two-handed swords, tall hide shields and javelins.

On the table, the Mauryan army is slow but hits hard. Hold the Dhanvin behind a line of Carmin, whose tall shields shrug off arrows, and give the archers rounds of Hold so their bows gain Strength, but keep them out of a Marsh. The Maula Retinue is a large block of armored household troops rather than a small guard. Elephants are your hammer, and both retinues can Rally them. Use the Āṭavika to ambush from wet ground, and keep their chiefs alive, or nobody will bring them back.
