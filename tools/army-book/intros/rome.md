c. 27 BC–AD 235 · From Augustus to the Severans

When Augustus ended the civil wars, he turned Rome's armies into a permanent professional force that guarded a frontier stretching from Britain to the Euphrates. For the next two centuries the legions fought Germans on the Rhine, Britons at Mons Graupius, Dacians in the mountains beyond the Danube and Parthians in the east, and they won far more often than they lost.

At the core of every army were the legionaries: Roman citizens who served for twenty-five years, drilled until they could march, build a fortified camp and fight in close order better than any enemy. Each carried two heavy javelins, the pila, a large curved shield and a short sword for close work. The legion threw its pila just before contact to break up the enemy's shields and formation, then closed in with the sword. Veterans, the emperor's Praetorian Guard and senior officers stiffened the line. Beside the legions served the auxilia, non-citizen troops who made up half the army and who often did most of the fighting. Their spearmen, archers and mixed cohorts of infantry and cavalry gave Rome the light troops and horsemen its legions lacked. Every legion also carried artillery, from bolt-shooting scorpiones to the cart-mounted carroballistae shown on Trajan's Column.

On the table, Rome wins by being steady. Its legionaries are well armoured, well trained and hard to break, and their pila hit hard just before the lines meet. Use the auxilia and cavalry to cover the flanks and the artillery to wear the enemy down as it approaches, then let the legions grind through whatever reaches them.
