c. 750–650 BC · From Piye's conquest of Egypt to the fall of Thebes

For a thousand years the Egyptians called Nubia Ta-Seti, the Land of the Bow, and hired its archers for their own wars. In the eighth century BC the kings of Napata, far up the Nile, turned the tables. Piye sailed north, purified his army before Amun at Thebes, broke the Libyan chiefs of the Delta, and made himself pharaoh of a united Egypt. For almost a century his family ruled from the Fourth Cataract to the Mediterranean as the 25th Dynasty. Taharqa, the greatest of them, raised temples across both lands, ran his soldiers through the desert from Memphis to the Faiyum to keep them hard, and fought the Assyrian Empire for twenty years before Ashurbanipal's armies drove his successor back to Nubia.

The Kushite army was an army of bowmen. Greek writers remembered their bows as taller than a man, and Egyptian art shows the archers with feathers in their hair. Around them stood Egyptian spearmen and recruits, Kushite warriors with maces and throwsticks, Libyan warriors from the Delta with long swords, and desert scouts in the old tradition of the Medjay. Kush was famous for its horses as well: Assyrian kings prized Kushite horses, and Piye himself wept to find the horses of a defeated rival left to starve.
