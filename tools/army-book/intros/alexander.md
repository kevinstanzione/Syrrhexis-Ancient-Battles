c. 359–168 BC · From Philip II to Pydna

When Philip II took the throne of Macedon in 359 BC, his kingdom was a backward state on the edge of the Greek world. In twenty years he turned it into the strongest power in Greece. He drilled his peasant infantry into a pike phalanx armed with the sarissa, a spear five metres long or more, and built around it a professional army of guards, light troops, engineers and noble cavalry. At Chaeronea in 338 BC it broke the armies of Athens and Thebes. His son Alexander took the same army across the Hellespont, beat the Persian Empire at the Granicus, Issus and Gaugamela, and marched it as far as India. After Alexander died in 323 BC, his generals fought one another for forty years over his empire, and the kingdoms they founded, Antigonid Macedon, the Seleucid east and Ptolemaic Egypt, fought in the Macedonian way until Rome ended them.

The army fought as a team. The Pezhétairoi, the "foot companions", formed a hedge of pikes that no enemy could face head on. The Hypaspistaí linked them to the cavalry, and the Agriânes and Cretan archers screened the whole line. The decisive blow came from the Hetaîroi, the Companion cavalry, charging in wedges with the king at their head while Thessalian horsemen held the other wing. The Successors added Indian elephants with fighting towers, Tarantine javelin cavalry, and the versatile Thureophóroi, and the old guard of Alexander's hypaspists fought on into their sixties as the Argyráspides, the Silver Shields.

On the table, the Armies of Alexander win with hammer and anvil. Your phalanx is slow and clumsy to turn, but nothing charges its front without paying for it, so use it to pin the enemy line. Keep your Hypaspistaí and light troops on its flanks, then strike with your Companions where the enemy is already held. Lead from the front: your king gains Initiative when he charges, but losing his retinue will cost you dearly.
