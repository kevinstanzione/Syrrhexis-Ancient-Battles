c. 1550–1070 BC · From the expulsion of the Hyksos to the end of the New Kingdom

The Hyksos kings who ruled northern Egypt brought the horse, the light chariot and the composite bow with them from Asia, and when Ahmose drove them out he kept all three. The pharaohs of the New Kingdom turned them into the most famous army of the Bronze Age. Thutmose III marched through the Megiddo pass to break a Canaanite coalition and campaigned in Syria seventeen times. Ramesses II fought the Hittites at Kadesh in 1274 BC, and Ramesses III threw back the Sea Peoples on land and in the mouths of the Nile.

The chariot corps was the army's elite. Each light, two-horse chariot carried a driver and a warrior with a composite bow, and the king led them in person in a chariot of his own. Runners went with them on foot to finish off what the arrows brought down. Behind came companies of spearmen with tall round-topped shields, shock troops with the curved khopesh and the axe, and massed bowmen shooting in ranks. Young recruits filled out the levy, and foreigners served beside the Egyptians: Sherden warriors with horned helmets and long swords guarded Ramesses II, and the Canaanite Ne'arin arrived at Kadesh just in time to save his camp.

On the table, Egypt wins with combined arms. The Snnyw chariots shoot and skirmish rather than ride infantry down, so let the Mnfyt and Nḫtw-ꜥ hold the line while the chariots wear the enemy down from range. The Styw are a cheap firing line, but they cannot skirmish: keep them behind or beside the spearmen. Send the Pḥrrw in after the chariots to finish units they have caught, and hold the Nꜥrn in Ambush for the moment the enemy commits. The Pr-ꜥꜣ Retinue carries the pharaoh himself, and Inspiring lets him give up his Hold to make one nearby unit strike more surely at the height of the fighting.
